## Recovery hierarchy: sleep and nutrition first, gadgets second

tags: recovery, sleep, tired, fatigue, "active-recovery", modalities, deload, rest

The evidence on recovery strategies places **sleep, nutrition, and periodisation as foundational** — described as "the cake" — while recovery devices (ice baths, foam rollers, massage guns, compression gear) are "the icing." Skipping fundamentals and investing in gadgets is a common mistake. In order of priority:

1. **Sleep (7–9 h/night):** The most powerful recovery tool available. Sleep loss impairs cognition, immunity, and performance.
2. **Nutrition:** Three pillars post-exercise — refuel (carbohydrates), rebuild (protein), rehydrate.
3. **Periodisation (deload/taper):** Structured training manipulation with planned reduced-load weeks is a fundamental, not optional, part of adaptation.
4. Then: modalities as adjuncts.

## What the evidence says about common recovery tools

tags: "cold-water", "ice-bath", "foam-rolling", "active-recovery", sore, soreness, "muscle-soreness", massage

**Cold water immersion (CWI):** Use water at **11–15°C for 11–15 minutes**. Effective for reducing pain and inflammation after intense training. However, **chronic use after resistance training may blunt muscle-building adaptations** — CWI suppresses the post-training inflammatory signals that drive hypertrophy. Use it for competition weeks or acute soreness management, not as a routine post-strength session habit. For a muscle-gain or recomposition goal, limit CWI in the days following heavy strength sessions.

**Foam rolling:** Optimal dose is **90–120 seconds per muscle group**. Reduces DOMS and improves range of motion. Evidence for direct performance gains is limited but soreness reduction is consistent. Easy to implement, low risk.

**Compression garments:** Most effective when worn **>24 hours post-resistance exercise**. Consistently reduces perceived muscle soreness; 71% of elite athletes use them at least once per week. Low risk, easy to wear overnight.

**Active recovery:** Light-to-moderate exercise well below the lactate threshold reduces soreness perception — an easy walk or bike ride the day after a hard session is often better than full rest. Evidence for performance outcomes is mixed, but for feeling better it works.

**Massage guns:** Only limited evidence; two case reports of serious injury (rhabdomyolysis, haemothorax) from improper use. Use cautiously.

**High-dose antioxidants (Vitamin C/E as supplements):** May blunt training adaptations — avoid routine supplementation at high doses specifically for recovery purposes (consistent with DOMS evidence from Schroeter et al. 2024).
