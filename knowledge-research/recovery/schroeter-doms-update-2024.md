# DOMS: Mechanisms, Time Course, and Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches

**Full citation:** Schroeter S, Bloch W, Hirschmüller A, Engelhardt M, Grim C, Heiss R, Hotfiel T. Update: Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS) – muscle biomechanics, pathophysiology and therapeutic approaches. *Dtsch Z Sportmed*. 2024;75:189-194. doi:10.5960/dzsm.2024.608  
**Source URL:** https://www.germanjournalsportsmedicine.com/archive/archive-2024/issue-5/update-delayed-onset-muscle-soreness-doms-muscle-biomechanics-pathophysiology-and-therapeutic-approaches/  
**Access:** Open access (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)

## What this source covers
An updated review of DOMS mechanisms (biomechanical, histological, inflammatory), its time course, damage biomarkers, and the evidence for various therapeutic and preventive approaches — including what does not work.

## Key findings & numbers

**Time course:**
- Initial symptoms appear **6–12 hours post-exercise**
- Peak severity: **48–72 hours** post-exercise
- Resolution: typically **within one week** without complications
- Trigger: mechanical overload exceeding ultrastructural capacity (not solely metabolic stress or lactic acid — the lactic acid theory is debunked)

**Pathophysiology:**
- Primary mechanism: ultrastructural injury patterns including intracellular oedema, myofibrillar breakdown, and T-tubule disruption
- Elevated biomarkers: creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP)
- Eccentric contractions are the dominant trigger (lengthening under load)

**Effective treatments:**
- **Cryotherapy (cold water immersion):** Reduces inflammatory processes; effective for pain/swelling
- **Heat therapy:** Effective for pain reduction and muscle tone normalisation through increased perfusion; particularly for heat patches/warm baths
- **Compression therapy:** Promising results; mechanism uncertain but practical to implement
- **Massage:** Evidence supports increased intramuscular perfusion and faster recovery
- **Low-intensity active recovery:** Increased blood flow accelerates debris clearance

**What does NOT work well:**
- Antioxidant supplements (Vitamin C, Vitamin E): may interfere with necessary cellular repair processes; no clear benefit and possible harm
- Gelatin: "no functional benefits in recovery"
- BCAA supplementation: evidence remains controversial — some studies show modest soreness reduction, others show none
- NSAIDs: dosage-dependent effects; timing of administration matters; chronic use may impair long-term adaptation

**Prevention:**
- Gradual exercise progression prevents severe DOMS onset
- The "repeated bout effect": prior exposure to eccentric exercise confers protection; subsequent bouts cause significantly less DOMS

## Direct specifics / quotes
> "Low-intensity training involving less load [with gradual progression] prevents exercise-induced muscle damage onset."

## Relevance to a body-recomposition coaching app
- DOMS peaks at 48–72 h: when a user logs high soreness 2 days after a hard session, this is expected physiology — reassure and advise light activity
- Soreness score (1–10) combined with session date allows the coach to identify DOMS vs. chronic overtraining
- Cold water immersion and heat therapy are practical, accessible DOMS remedies; both have evidence
- Active recovery (easy walk, light bike) is the best readily available DOMS treatment — better than complete rest
- Warning new users that first few weeks of resistance training will involve DOMS reduces dropout ("this is normal, not injury")
- Antioxidant supplements are commonly taken but may not help with DOMS and could blunt training adaptations — coach should not recommend them for soreness relief
