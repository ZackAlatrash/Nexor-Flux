## What is the difference between overreaching and overtraining syndrome

tags: overtraining, overreaching, burnout, "under-recovery", fatigue, tired, deload, recovery, stress

There are three distinct states of excessive training load, each with a different recovery timeline:

**Functional overreaching (FOR):** Short-term performance drop from intensified training that resolves within **days to 2 weeks** with rest. This is a normal, desirable part of training — the body is being pushed and will supercompensate. A hard training week followed by a deload is intentional FOR.

**Nonfunctional overreaching (NFOR):** Prolonged training stress producing maladaptations that require **months** of recovery. Performance does not fully recover between blocks. Requires significant training reduction or temporary cessation.

**Overtraining syndrome (OTS):** Accumulation of training AND non-training stress (poor sleep, low calories, psychological load) causing long-term performance decrements lasting **several weeks to months**. The treatment is rest with optimal nutrition — timeline is unpredictable. Research shows **64% of female and 66% of male** elite distance runners reported experiencing staleness at some point in their careers. OTS is not rare.

## How to recognise overtraining before it becomes serious

tags: overtraining, overreaching, burnout, fatigue, tired, "low-energy", stress, deload

Warning signs that distinguish chronic overreaching from normal training fatigue:
- Generalised fatigue and low energy that doesn't resolve with a day or two of rest
- Insomnia or disrupted sleep despite feeling exhausted
- Loss of appetite or change in appetite
- Irritability, loss of motivation, difficulty concentrating
- Declining training performance over **2+ consecutive weeks** despite consistent training
- Frequent minor infections (suppressed immune function)

Key risk factors that compound the risk: **insufficient calorie intake, low protein/carbohydrate intake, poor sleep quality, and high psychological stress** outside training. Recomposition athletes — running a calorie deficit while training hard — face elevated OTS risk because low energy availability is a primary trigger.

A performance decrement lasting **>2 weeks** is the practical threshold to act: this meets the criteria for at minimum nonfunctional overreaching. At this point, add a structured deload (reduce volume 40–60%, keep intensity the same) and optimise sleep and nutrition before resuming progressive overload.

Tracking energy score (1–10), soreness, and mood over time reveals overreaching patterns early — these subjective markers (Profile of Mood States: fatigue up, vigor down, tension up) are among the most consistently reported diagnostic signals across studies (Carrard et al. 2022). Blood tests and resting hormones are often normal even in overtrained athletes — don't wait for lab confirmation to act.

## What is a deload and how to do one

tags: deload, taper, recovery, recover, fatigue, overtraining, readiness, rest

A deload is a planned reduction in training load to allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate while preserving fitness. Evidence from endurance tapering research (Wang et al. 2023, meta-analysis of 14 studies) confirms the optimal approach: **reduce volume by 41–60%** (approximately half) while **keeping training intensity (weights used) and frequency the same**. This is best done over **8–14 days** for maximum benefit — a standard 1-week deload sits within the optimal range.

Key reassurance for users afraid of losing gains: VO₂max and strength are maintained during a 1-week deload. Performance typically **improves** after a deload, not decreases — fatigue is cleared, revealing underlying fitness that was masked. You are not going backwards during a deload; you are allowing your body to express the gains you've already built.

Schedule a deload every **4–8 weeks** of progressive training, or any time energy scores drop below your personal average for 5–7 consecutive days.
