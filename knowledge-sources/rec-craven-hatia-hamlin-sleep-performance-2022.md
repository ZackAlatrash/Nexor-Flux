## How much does one bad night of sleep hurt training

tags: sleep, performance, tired, fatigue, strength, power, output, hours

A meta-analysis of 77 studies (Craven et al. 2022) found that acute sleep loss reduces overall physical performance by an average of **−7.6%**. The worst-hit category is skill tasks (technique-heavy movements, court sports): **−20.9%**. Anaerobic power drops **−6.3%**, high-intensity interval performance **−6.2%**, endurance **−5.6%**, and maximal strength **−2.9%**. Performance declines approximately **0.4% for every additional hour awake** after sleep deprivation. Afternoon/evening training is hurt more than morning sessions — if you slept poorly, train in the morning when the impact is smallest.

After a single night of only 4 hours, average and maximum power output on anaerobic cycle tests declines measurably (Hatia 2024). For recomposition, a consistent pattern of short sleep means your workouts are regularly operating at a quality deficit, limiting the stimulus for muscle growth and calorie burn.

## Sleep under 8 hours raises injury risk significantly

tags: sleep, injury, hours, recovery, tired, fatigue, performance, readiness

Athletes sleeping under 8 hours per night are **1.7 times more likely to sustain an injury** (Hatia 2024, adolescent cohort). Hamlin et al. (2021), tracking 82 university athletes daily over an academic year, found that sleeping ≥8 hours was associated with **20% lower injury and illness risk** (OR = 0.8). Sleep quality adds even more protection — athletes reporting good sleep quality had **40% lower injury/illness risk** (OR = 0.6) compared to those with poor quality sleep, regardless of hours.

So both quantity (≥8 h) and quality (feeling rested) matter independently. Energy and mood also improve: sleeping ≥8 hours was associated with higher energy (OR = 1.2) and better perceived training quality (OR = 1.3). These are concrete, measurable benefits, not vague wellness claims.

## Sleep extension and napping: the most effective sleep interventions

tags: sleep, nap, napping, recovery, tired, hours, "sleep-quality", performance

The most effective sleep interventions for athletes are simply sleeping more and strategic napping (Cunha et al. 2023, systematic review of 25 studies). Extending sleep by **46–113 minutes per night** in people habitually getting ~7 hours produces consistent performance improvements across all included studies. A **20–90-minute nap** can restore performance that was impaired by the previous night's partial sleep restriction, and also improves performance even after a normal sleep night. Sleep hygiene advice (screens off, consistent schedule) has weaker direct performance evidence but is worthwhile as a habit foundation. Light therapy (morning bright light, evening blue-light blocking) shows promise for circadian alignment. Start with the simplest fix: go to bed 30 minutes earlier.
