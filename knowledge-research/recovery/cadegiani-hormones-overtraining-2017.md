# Hormonal Diagnostic Markers for Overtraining Syndrome: Stimulation Tests More Reliable than Basal Levels

**Full citation:** Cadegiani FA, Kater CE. Hormonal aspects of overtraining syndrome: a systematic review. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil*. 2017;9:14. doi:10.1186/s13102-017-0079-8  
**PMCID:** PMC5541747  
**Source URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5541747/  
**Access:** Open access (CC BY 4.0)

## What this source covers
A systematic review of 38 articles (from 835 initial results) examining hormonal alterations associated with overtraining syndrome (OTS), functional overreaching (FOR), and nonfunctional overreaching (NFOR), with particular focus on diagnostic value of basal vs. stimulated hormone measurements.

## Key findings & numbers
- **Key finding:** Basal (resting) hormone levels are largely **not effective diagnostic markers** — "basal levels of hormones were mostly normal in athletes with OTS/FOR/NFOR compared with healthy athletes" (at least 75% of studies showed normal values for each of 17 tested hormones)

**Growth hormone (GH):**
- Blunted stimulation response in **57.1%** of studies
- Most pronounced when tested through maximal exercise protocols

**ACTH:**
- Blunted stimulation response in **57.1%** of studies
- Maximal exercise stimulation test is the recommended method

**Testosterone/Cortisol (T/C) ratio:**
- Altered in **50%** of studies; reduced in **40%** — the most consistent *basal* finding
- However, not reliable enough alone for diagnosis

**Cortisol:**
- Inconsistent findings across studies ("conflicting results")

**Catecholamines (adrenaline/noradrenaline):**
- Inconsistent across studies; nocturnal elevation observed in some OTS cases (Carrard review)

**Prolactin:**
- Blunted response in some studies; may be useful as supplementary marker

**Practical diagnostic conclusion:**
- "Blunted ACTH and GH responses to stimulation tests may be good predictors of OTS/FOR/NFOR"
- Stimulation tests (e.g., maximal exercise test) are more diagnostically meaningful than routine blood panels
- No single hormone test definitively diagnoses OTS — remains a clinical diagnosis supported by multiple markers

## Relevance to a body-recomposition coaching app
- Explains why a standard blood panel won't definitively diagnose overtraining — resting testosterone and cortisol levels are often normal even in overtrained athletes
- The testosterone/cortisol (T/C) ratio trend over time is more useful than a single measurement — if users track mood, recovery, and training quality, the app can infer anabolic/catabolic state directionally
- Practical implication: when in doubt about overtraining, use subjective wellness metrics (energy, mood, soreness) combined with performance regression data rather than waiting for lab tests
- For coaches advising users: fatigue + performance decline + mood changes + poor sleep over 2+ weeks = act on deload, don't wait for lab confirmation
- Hormonal disruption from overtraining also impacts body composition directly: low T/C environment favours muscle catabolism and fat accumulation — relevant for recomposition goals
