## Poor sleep makes you eat 300 extra calories a day without realising it

tags: sleep, appetite, hunger, intake, tired

In a controlled 21-day inpatient crossover study comparing 4-hour vs 9-hour sleep opportunity, sleep-restricted adults consumed **308 additional calories per day** (about 17% more than baseline) with unlimited food access. Critically, their energy expenditure did not change at all — resting metabolic rate, thermic effect of food, and activity expenditure were identical between conditions. Every extra calorie eaten was surplus. Over the restriction period, sleep-restricted subjects gained **0.5 kg more weight** and accumulated **+11% more visceral fat** (+7.8 cm²). After returning to normal sleep, calorie intake normalised within 3 days — but visceral fat continued accumulating for longer.

If you are tracking calories carefully but still gaining fat or weight, poor sleep may be causing unconscious overconsumption. You don't feel like you're eating more — but the data shows otherwise. Getting 7–9 hours is a direct tool for keeping calorie intake in range.

## Late-night eating from poor sleep adds 550+ extra calories

tags: sleep, appetite, hunger, intake, tired

In a 5-night sleep restriction experiment (Spaeth et al. 2013, N=225), subjects sleeping only 4 hours/night consumed **553 additional calories** during the late-night window (22:00–03:59). These late-night calories came disproportionately from higher-fat foods (33% fat vs 28% during daytime). Sleep-restricted subjects gained significantly more weight than controls. The mechanism is extended wakefulness: more hours awake = more opportunities and lower impulse control for eating. The simplest intervention is also the most powerful — **go to bed earlier**, which eliminates the late-night eating window entirely.
