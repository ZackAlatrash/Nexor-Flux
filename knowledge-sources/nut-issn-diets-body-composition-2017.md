## Energy balance — the foundation of fat loss and muscle gain
tags: calories, calorie, energy, energy-balance, tdee, deficit, surplus, fat, loss, lean, macros
All diet approaches — low-fat, low-carb, ketogenic, intermittent fasting — produce comparable fat loss and muscle gain results **when total calories and protein are equated**. The ISSN position stand is explicit: no diet style has been shown to produce superior body composition outcomes after controlling for energy intake and protein. The implication for coaching: calorie target and protein intake are the levers that matter; the macro split is a compliance tool.

## How fast to lose weight — recommended rate of fat loss
tags: weight, rate, fat, loss, lose, cut, cutting, lean, muscle, sustainable, scale
A fat loss rate of **0.5–1.0% of body weight per week** preserves lean mass for most individuals. Faster loss (>1%/week) risks disproportionate muscle breakdown, especially in already-lean people. Larger caloric surpluses (up to 544–2,010 kcal/day above maintenance) are appropriate for untrained beginners building muscle fast; trained athletes should use much smaller surpluses to avoid excess fat gain.

## Ketogenic diet for fat loss — what the evidence actually shows
tags: keto, carb, low-carb, cut, cutting, fat, loss, calories, calorie, macros
Every controlled study that matched protein and calories between a ketogenic diet and a standard diet found **no fat-loss advantage for keto**. Under ad libitum (eat freely) conditions, keto produced about **294 kcal/day spontaneous intake reduction** — a real appetite-suppression effect, but not a metabolic magic trick. Keto may impair high-intensity performance (strength, power) due to reduced pyruvate dehydrogenase activity. Choose keto if it helps you adhere to your calorie target; do not choose it expecting faster fat loss from the diet type itself.

## Metabolic adaptation — why dieting gets harder over time
tags: plateau, metabolic-adaptation, diet-break, deficit, calories, calories, tdee, weight, fat, loss
Adaptive thermogenesis reduces total energy expenditure by **10–15% beyond** what tissue loss alone predicts when someone loses ≥10% of body weight. This means calorie targets must be periodically revised downward as weight is lost — a target set at the start of a diet will over-estimate TDEE after significant weight loss. Higher protein intake and resistance training are the best-evidenced strategies to blunt this adaptation. The Biggest Loser data (Fothergill 2016) documents adaptation persisting for years; the MATADOR study (Byrne 2018) shows structured diet breaks attenuate it.
