## Progressive overload: adding reps works just as well as adding weight
tags: "progressive-overload", progression, overload, progress, hypertrophy, muscle, strength, gains

Progressive overload is the single most important training principle for continued muscle and strength gains — but there are two equally valid ways to apply it. This 8-week RCT (43 resistance-trained participants) compared (a) increasing load each week vs (b) increasing reps each week and found **both groups gained appreciable muscle mass (6.7–12.9 % across sites)** with no practically significant difference between them. Strength gains also did not differ meaningfully (the load-progression group gained ~2 kg more on squat 1RM, within measurement noise). You do not need to add weight every session. Either progress — more reps OR more weight — signals sufficient overload for the muscle to grow.

## Double progression: the practical rep-range method
tags: "progressive-overload", progression, overload, progress, reps, "rep-range", load, hypertrophy, muscle

The most practical application of progressive overload for natural trainees is **double progression**: set a target rep range (e.g., 8–12 reps), then — once you hit the top of the range on all sets — increase the load by the smallest available increment (typically 2.5–5 kg) and work back up to the top of the range again. This approach combines both forms of overload (reps and load) in sequence, prevents stagnation, and gives clear objective milestones. The Plotkin 2022 data support this: both rep-progression and load-progression produce near-identical hypertrophy, so there is no need to force weight increases before you are ready. What matters is that something progressively increases over time — track both weight and reps each session so you have clear evidence of overload.
