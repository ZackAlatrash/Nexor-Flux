## What is DOMS and when does it peak

tags: sore, soreness, doms, ache, "muscle-soreness", recovery, tired, fatigue

Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) is caused by mechanical overload — particularly **eccentric contractions** (lowering weight, downhill running) — that exceeds the ultrastructural capacity of muscle fibres, causing micro-damage and an inflammatory response. It has nothing to do with lactic acid, which has been debunked as the cause.

The timeline: initial symptoms appear **6–12 hours after exercise**; soreness peaks at **48–72 hours** post-exercise; and fully resolves within **one week** in most cases without complications. So the soreness you feel two days after a hard leg session is normal physiology, not injury. Elevated biomarkers (creatine kinase, IL-6, CRP) confirm the inflammatory phase is real but temporary.

## What actually helps soreness vs what doesn't

tags: sore, soreness, doms, "ice-bath", "cold-water", "active-recovery", massage, "foam-rolling"

**Effective for DOMS:**
- **Cold water immersion (cryotherapy):** Reduces inflammation and pain/swelling. Use for soreness relief after very hard sessions — but see the Driller caution about chronic use after resistance training (below).
- **Heat therapy:** Warm baths and heat patches reduce pain and normalise muscle tone by increasing blood perfusion.
- **Massage:** Increases intramuscular blood flow; evidence supports faster recovery. Practical for targeted sore muscles.
- **Low-intensity active recovery:** Light walking, easy cycling, or swimming accelerates debris clearance via increased blood flow. This is the most accessible and consistently recommended option.
- **Compression garments:** Promising evidence for reducing perceived soreness post-resistance training.

**Does NOT help DOMS well:**
- **Antioxidant supplements (Vitamin C, Vitamin E):** May interfere with the necessary cellular repair processes — no clear benefit and possible harm to training adaptation. Do not take antioxidant supplements specifically to manage DOMS.
- **Gelatin:** No functional recovery benefit.
- **BCAA supplements:** Evidence is controversial — some studies show modest benefit, others none.
- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen etc.):** Timing and dosage matter; chronic use may impair long-term training adaptation.

The best defence against severe DOMS is **gradual progression**: increasing training load incrementally prevents the muscle damage that triggers it. After a first exposure, subsequent bouts cause significantly less soreness ("repeated bout effect").
