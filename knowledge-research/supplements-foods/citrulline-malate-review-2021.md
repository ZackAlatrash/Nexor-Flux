# Critical Review: Citrulline Malate Supplementation and Exercise Performance (2021)

**Full citation:** Gough LA, Sparks SA, McNaughton LR, et al. A critical review of citrulline malate supplementation and exercise performance. Eur J Appl Physiol. 2021;121(12):3283-3295. doi: 10.1007/s00421-021-04774-6
**Source URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8571142/
**Access:** open access (CC BY 4.0)

## What this source covers
Critical evaluation of evidence for citrulline malate (CM) supplementation on exercise performance, examining proposed mechanisms (nitric oxide enhancement, ammonia clearance, malate-aspartate shuttle), acute dosing studies, and comparison with L-citrulline. Critically examines manufacturer quality control issues.

## Key findings & numbers
- Standard acute dose: 8 g CM administered ~60 minutes before exercise
- Peak plasma citrulline: ~60 min post-ingestion, declining rapidly 15–30 min thereafter
- Higher doses studied: 10–15 g; 15 g achieves 3849 ± 190 μmol/L plasma vs. 10 g: 2756 ± 170 μmol/L
- Resistance exercise — positive findings: 8 g CM improved bench press repetitions in later sets (Perez-Guisado 2010); increased repetitions to failure in leg press set 5, hack squat sets 4–5, push-ups (Wax 2015/2016); improved bench press and leg press in trained females (Glenn 2017)
- Resistance exercise — null findings: no benefit during German Volume Training 10×10 at 80% 1RM (Chappell 2018)
- Effect sizes for positive resistance studies: 0.23–0.59 (described as "subtle")
- Cycling/aerobic — null findings: 8 g CM showed no improvement in 10×15-second sprints (Cunniffe 2016); no effect at 90% VO2peak time-to-tolerance (314 s vs. 315 s placebo, p=0.94) (Gills 2021)
- Blood flow: 8 g CM 2 hours pre-exercise showed no effect on muscle blood flow (3.78 vs. 3.72 ml/min/100ml, p=NS; Trexler 2019)
- NO levels: not significantly elevated in human exercise studies
- Recovery: 6 g CM showed no improvement in muscular endurance recovery at 24, 48, 72 hours post-exercise (da Silva 2017)
- Chronic use: 2 g/day for 8 weeks produced no changes in maximal strength (Hwang 2018)
- Quality control issue: manufacturer-stated 2:1 citrulline:malate ratio consistently lower in testing (actual 1.1:1 to 1.6:1); 8 g at 1.1:1 ratio delivers only ~4.2 g citrulline instead of intended 5.3 g
- Safety: no significant GI distress at doses up to 12 g; no adverse effects on blood markers

## Direct specifics / quotes
> "Until such studies are completed, the efficacy of CM supplementation to improve exercise performance remains ambiguous."

> "From the available evidence, an acute 8 g dose CM may, albeit not consistently, increase muscular endurance-strength performance. However, there is little evidence to advocate its use for production and maintenance of muscular power, maximal strength, recovery of muscular function, or supporting muscular adaptations."

## Relevance to a body-recomposition coaching app
- Evidence strength: MODERATE-TO-LOW overall; most consistent (but still equivocal) evidence is for muscular endurance in resistance training; not supported for aerobic endurance, power, or maximal strength
- Mechanisms proposed (NO, ammonia clearance, malate shuttle) are not well-confirmed in human studies
- L-citrulline alone may be a better-evidenced option than citrulline malate; meta-analysis on L-citrulline separately (see citrulline-endurance-meta-2023.md) shows no endurance benefit
- Quality control issues with commercial products are a practical concern
- Coaches should present CM as "may help with late-set muscular endurance" rather than a guaranteed benefit
- Safe to use; main issue is insufficient consistent evidence
