# Three Mechanisms of Muscle Hypertrophy: Tension, Stress, Damage

**Full citation:** Schoenfeld BJ. The mechanisms of muscle hypertrophy and their application to resistance training. J Strength Cond Res. 2010;24(10):2857–2872. doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181e840f3. PMID:20847704.
**Source URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20847704/
**Access:** abstract-only (JSCR is subscription journal; abstract from PubMed)

## What this source covers
Foundational review paper identifying and explaining the three primary mechanisms by which resistance training stimulates muscle hypertrophy; foundational paper highly cited in all subsequent hypertrophy research.

## Key findings & numbers
- **Three primary mechanisms of muscle hypertrophy:**
  1. **Mechanical tension:** Physical forces on muscle fibers during contraction and stretching; considered the PRIMARY driver. Activates satellite cells and muscle protein synthesis pathways (mTOR).
  2. **Metabolic stress:** Accumulation of metabolites (lactate, hydrogen ions, phosphate) during high-rep/short-rest training. Associated with cell swelling ("the pump") and anabolic hormone release.
  3. **Muscle damage:** Microtrauma from eccentric contractions and novel movements, triggering inflammatory repair and satellite cell activation.
- Bodybuilders (moderate loads, brief rest, metabolic stress) and powerlifters (heavy loads, long rest, mechanical tension) both achieve hypertrophy through different mechanism emphasis.
- The review posits that "an optimal hypertrophy program should integrate training variables to take advantage of each of these mechanisms."
- Practical application: vary rep ranges, load, and rest periods to maximize all three pathways.

## Direct specifics / quotes
> "Mechanical tension, muscle damage, and metabolic stress all can play a role in exercise-induced muscle growth."

## Relevance to a body-recomposition coaching app
- The three-mechanism framework justifies varied training approaches: not just "lift heavy" OR "high reps."
- Mechanical tension (heavy compound lifts) is likely the dominant driver — always prioritize progressive overload.
- Metabolic stress (higher rep sets, shorter rest, the "pump") has a supporting role — useful for volume finishers.
- Muscle damage (eccentric emphasis, novel exercises, long muscle length) contributes — relevant when introducing new exercises.
- Coaches should program a blend: heavy sets (3–5 reps), moderate hypertrophy sets (6–15 reps), and occasional higher-rep metabolic sets (15–30 reps).
