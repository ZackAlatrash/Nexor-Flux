## Which protein source is best — DIAAS quality rankings
tags: "protein-quality", diaas, pdcaas, whey, sources, "amino-acids", leucine, "plant-based"
Evidence strength: STRONG methodological consensus; DIAAS is the FAO-preferred protein quality standard since 2013.

DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score) measures how well each amino acid in a protein is actually absorbed and used by the body — it is more accurate than the older PDCAAS method, which used fecal digestibility and capped scores at 100%.

**DIAAS scores for common protein sources:**
| Source | DIAAS | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Whey protein concentrate | 133% | Highest quality; 11% leucine content |
| Egg | 122% | Near-ideal EAA profile |
| Whey protein isolate | 125% | |
| Soy protein isolate | 98% | Best plant-based option |
| Pea protein concentrate | 82% | Limited by sulfur amino acids |
| Rice protein | 60% | Limited by lysine |

**Animal flesh (chicken, fish, beef, turkey):** effectively 100% — all amino acids fully available.

**Key rule:** protein complementarity must happen at the meal level, not across the day. A lysine-deficient cereal breakfast cannot be corrected by a high-quality dinner. Pair plant proteins within the same meal (e.g., rice + beans, pea + rice protein blend).

## How much protein do plant-based and vegetarian athletes need
tags: plant-based, vegan, vegetarian, protein-quality, diaas, sources
Evidence strength: STRONG (consistent finding across DIAAS literature and ISSN protein stand).

Because plant proteins score lower on DIAAS than animal proteins, vegetarian and vegan athletes must eat more total grams to deliver the same amino acid stimulus for muscle protein synthesis.

**Practical guidance:**
- Vegetarian/vegan athletes: target **1.8–2.4 g/kg/day** total protein (vs. ~1.6–2.0 g/kg/day for omnivores).
- Need 20–40% more plant protein grams to match the EAA and leucine delivery of animal protein.
- A 2019 study found vegetarian athletes had 43% less "available protein" (DIAAS-adjusted) than omnivores eating the same gram count — the digestibility gap, not just the quantity gap, matters.
- Soy protein isolate is the exception (~98% DIAAS) — treat it nearly on par with whey.
- Corn-based foods (tortillas, corn cereals) are particularly poor protein sources due to severe lysine limitation (~66% DIAAS).

**For a plant-based athlete needing 160 g protein/day (animal equivalent):** target approximately 190–224 g/day from plant sources.

## Best high-protein foods ranked by density
tags: high-protein, food, foods, sources, chicken, eggs, food-database
Evidence strength: STRONG; data sourced from USDA FoodData Central and ISSN 2024 protein review.

**Top animal-source high-protein foods (per 100 g cooked/as-served):**
- Cooked skinless chicken breast: 32 g protein, 158 kcal — best protein-to-calorie ratio.
- Turkey breast (cooked): ~30 g protein, ~160 kcal.
- Canned tuna (in water): ~26 g protein, ~110 kcal.
- Eggs (whole, large): ~13 g protein per 100 g (~6 g per egg), ~143 kcal/100 g.
- Cheddar cheese: 23 g protein per 100 g but 409 kcal — calorie-dense; use sparingly when cutting.
- Peanut butter: 24 g protein per 100 g but 632 kcal — not a lean protein source.

**Key takeaway for recomp:** lean meats (chicken breast, turkey, white fish) deliver the most protein per calorie, making them the most useful for hitting high protein targets while managing overall calorie intake.
