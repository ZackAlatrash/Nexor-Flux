## How much sleep do I need to recover and perform

tags: sleep, rest, hours, tired, fatigue, "sleep-quality", recovery, "how-much"

The National Sleep Foundation's expert panel (18 members from 12 scientific organisations, reviewing 312 articles) recommends **7–9 hours per night** for adults aged 18–64, and **7–8 hours** for adults 65 and over. Teenagers (14–17) need **8–10 hours**. Sleeping **less than 6 hours** is in the "not recommended" zone for all adults — associated with attention problems, increased injury risk, obesity, and diabetes. Anything **above 10–11 hours** for adults is also considered outside the healthy range.

For active people training regularly, the upper end of the range (8–9 h) is more appropriate. Athletes specifically may need **8–10 hours** per the 2021 IOC/BJSM consensus (Walsh et al.), as intense training raises recovery demand. If you are chronically sleeping under 7 hours, improving sleep duration is one of the highest-leverage actions you can take for recovery, body composition, and long-term health — more impactful than most supplements or training tweaks.

## Is 6 hours of sleep enough if I feel okay

tags: sleep, hours, tired, fatigue, rest, "sleep-quality", "low-energy", energy

Six hours sits at or below the lower boundary of recommended adult sleep duration. The NSF panel rates it as "may be appropriate" for a minority of individuals, but "not recommended" as a regular habit. The challenge is that people adapt subjectively to chronic sleep restriction — they stop feeling as sleepy but objective performance and recovery markers remain impaired. If you regularly get 6 hours and wonder why your energy scores are low, training feels harder than expected, or fat loss has stalled, sleep extension (moving bedtime 30–60 minutes earlier) is the first thing to address.
