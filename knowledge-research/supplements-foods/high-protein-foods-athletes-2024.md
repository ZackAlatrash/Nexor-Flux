# Common Protein Supplementation Misconceptions — JISSN 2024

**Full citation:** Antonio J, Evans C, Ferrando AA, et al. Common questions and misconceptions about protein supplementation: what does the scientific evidence really show? J Int Soc Sports Nutr. 2024;21(1):2341903. doi: 10.1080/15502783.2024.2341903
**Source URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11022925/
**Access:** open access (CC BY 4.0)

## What this source covers
Evidence-based review addressing common misconceptions about protein supplementation for athletes and exercising individuals. Covers protein food composition data, anabolic ceiling per meal, plant vs. animal protein, safety, and dose recommendations. Published 2024.

## Key findings & numbers

### Protein Content of Specific Foods
- Cooked skinless chicken breast (100 g): 32.1 g protein, 3.24 g fat, 158 kcal
- Cheddar cheese (100 g): 23.3 g protein, 34 g fat, 409 kcal
- Peanut butter (100 g): 24 g protein, 49.4 g fat, 632 kcal
- Protein density note: lean meats (chicken breast, turkey, fish) are the most protein-dense whole foods relative to calorie content

### Per-Meal Dosing
- Classic recommendation: 20–40 g per meal to maximize MPS
- Newer evidence: "the acute consumption of 100 grams [of protein] is indeed utilized by the body" — challenges prior belief that large single doses are wasted
- The body does not simply "waste" excess protein per meal; oxidation and splanchnic uptake handle overfeeding but whole-body protein synthesis is still stimulated

### Plant vs. Animal Protein
- Plant proteins "are absorbed at slower rates due to their incomplete amino acid profile and lower EAA content"
- Athletes on plant-based diets may need "20–40% more plant protein than animal-based protein to provide similar amounts of EAA and leucine"
- Practical implication: a vegan needing 160 g protein/day (animal equivalent) may need 190–224 g plant protein/day

### Protein Safety
- High-protein diets well-tolerated in healthy individuals, including those with normal kidney function
- No evidence of renal harm in healthy people at intakes up to 3+ g/kg/day
- Concern about renal harm applies only to individuals with pre-existing kidney disease

### Leucine Threshold
- Leucine is the key EAA trigger for MPS; higher leucine content per gram of protein = more anabolic signal per serving
- Whey protein is highest (~11% leucine); supports the "leucine threshold" model (~2–3 g leucine per meal)

## Direct specifics / quotes
> "Plant-based proteins are absorbed at slower rates due to their incomplete amino acid profile and lower EAA content; athletes following plant-based diets may need 20–40% more plant protein than animal-based protein to provide similar amounts of EAA and leucine."

## Relevance to a body-recomposition coaching app
- Evidence strength: STRONG (review of current evidence by ISSN authors)
- Practical food protein density for coaching: cooked chicken breast is ~32 g/100 g — useful reference
- For users asking about high-protein foods: chicken breast, turkey, fish (tuna, salmon), eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, whey protein are the most protein-dense relative to calorie load
- The updated "100 g is not wasted" finding suggests flexible meal timing is acceptable; emphasize total daily target over strict per-meal limits
- Plant-based users: increase gram targets by 20–40% to compensate for lower EAA density
- Protein is safe at high intakes for healthy users — no need to artificially cap recommendations for athletes
